SECTION 1: PROJECT SUMMARY/ABSTRACT
========================================================================

Relevance:

The α9α10 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) is an emerging
therapeutic target for chronic pain, inflammatory conditions, and
neuropsychiatric disorders. Unlike the extensively studied α7 nAChR,
the α9α10 subtype remains pharmacologically underexplored, with no
FDA-approved modulators and limited structure-activity relationship
(SAR) data. Positive allosteric modulators (PAMs) offer a promising
mechanism to enhance cholinergic signaling with improved selectivity.
However, the lack of large datasets for this target creates a
fundamental barrier to conventional machine learning approaches.
This project addresses this gap by developing a novel computational
framework—Transferable Activity Features (TAFs)—that extracts
interpretable, stereochemistry-dependent molecular features from
small datasets and transfers them across structurally distinct
scaffolds to discover novel active chemotypes.

Project Description:

This application proposes a five-year computational-experimental
drug discovery program with three integrated aims:

AIM 1: Develop and validate the TAF discovery framework using a
starting dataset of 30 α9α10 PAM compounds (7 active, 23 inactive).
We will identify activity-associated molecular features through
convergence of multiple independent methods—molecular fingerprint
analysis, matched molecular pair analysis, counterfactual perturbation,
feature ablation, and 3D shape analysis—and construct a scaffold-
independent TAF blueprint. Preliminary data from our pilot study
demonstrate five candidate TAFs: (1) a five-membered lactone core,
(2) a hydroxyl group, (3) an electron-withdrawing substituent critical
for high potency, (4) defined stereochemistry, and (5) molecular
weight 175-300 Da. Counterfactual analysis shows that removal of
the ethynyl group from the most potent compound (ID 12, 0.1981 uM)
results in predicted inactivation (88.5% confidence), providing
computational evidence for the importance of electron-withdrawing
groups.

AIM 2: Identify novel α9α10 PAM scaffolds through computationally
guided library screening and generative molecular design. We will:
(a) screen purchasable chemical databases (ZINC, Enamine REAL) for
TAF-compatible molecules using multi-objective scoring; (b) generate
novel scaffolds using REINVENT 4 with TAF-constrained scoring
functions; and (c) construct a diversified candidate portfolio
including positive candidates, mechanistic test compounds, stereo-
chemical pairs, and negative controls. All candidates will be
evaluated using a common assessment pipeline integrating TAF
compatibility, 3D shape matching, stereochemical analysis, novelty,
diversity, and uncertainty quantification.

AIM 3: Experimentally validate TAF transferability using a prospective
active learning loop. We will: (a) synthesize and test 15-20 selected
candidates using two-electrode voltage-clamp electrophysiology at
α9α10 nAChR-expressing Xenopus oocytes; (b) update TAF confidence
and transferability metrics with new experimental data; and (c)
iterate through the discovery cycle to refine the TAF blueprint.
The primary success criterion is demonstrating activity in at least
two chemically distinct scaffolds that were not present in the
training set.

Expected Outcomes:

This project will deliver: (1) a validated computational framework
for TAF discovery from small datasets; (2) a publicly available
dataset of novel α9α10 PAM chemotypes; (3) experimentally validated
TAF transferability metrics; and (4) a prospective lead compound
for preclinical development. The TAF approach addresses a critical
gap in AI-driven drug discovery—the inability to make reliable
predictions from small datasets—and provides a generalizable
methodology applicable to any pharmacological target with limited
experimental data.

Impact:

Success in this project will demonstrate that small, well-designed
datasets can be sufficient to identify transferable molecular features
for novel drug targets, challenging the prevailing assumption that
large datasets are required for AI-driven drug discovery. The TAF
framework will be applicable to other underexplored targets including
rare diseases, emerging infectious diseases, and neglected tropical
diseases where experimental data is inherently limited.

Keywords: α9α10 nAChR, positive allosteric modulators, transferable
activity features, scaffold hopping, few-shot learning, molecular
descriptors, cheminformatics, stereochemistry, drug discovery,
computational chemistry

SECTION 2: SPECIFIC AIMS
========================================================================

The α9α10 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) is a ligand-gated
ion channel expressed in dorsal root ganglia, cochlear hair cells, and
immune cells. It has been implicated in chronic pain, inflammation,
and neuropsychiatric disorders. Positive allosteric modulators (PAMs)
of α9α10 nAChR enhance cholinergic signaling without direct receptor
activation, offering a mechanism for improved selectivity and reduced
side effects compared to orthosteric agonists.

Despite its therapeutic potential, α9α10 nAChR remains
pharmacologically underexplored. Current SAR data is limited to
approximately 30 compounds with 7 confirmed active molecules. This
represents a critical barrier: conventional machine learning requires
large datasets, while traditional medicinal chemistry relies on
intuition that may not generalize. Neither approach is adequate for
a target with such limited experimental data.

We propose an alternative paradigm: Transferable Activity Features
(TAFs). Instead of building predictive models, we identify molecular
features consistently associated with activity that can be transferred
acstructurally distinct scaffolds. A TAF is defined as: chemical
feature + physicochemical identity + 3D location + spatial relationship
+ stereochemical context.

CENTRAL HYPOTHESIS:

Potency-associated, stereochemistry-dependent molecular features
learned from a small congeneric α9α10 PAM series can be transferred
across structurally distinct scaffolds to identify novel experimentally
active α9α10 PAM chemotypes.

This hypothesis is based on our preliminary data demonstrating:
(1) five candidate TAFs identified through convergence of multiple
independent methods; (2) counterfactual evidence that electron-
withdrawing groups are critical for high potency; and (3) 3D shape
analysis showing active compounds have distinct elongated conformations.

SPECIFIC AIM 1: Develop and validate the TAF discovery framework
for α9α10 PAMs.

Sub-aim 1a: Perform comprehensive cheminformatics analysis of the
starting dataset including molecular fingerprint generation, pairwise
similarity computation, principal component analysis, hierarchical
clustering, and Murcko scaffold decomposition.

Sub-aim 1b: Identify activity-associated features through substructure
enrichment analysis, matched molecular pair analysis, counterfactual
perturbation, and feature ablation.

Sub-aim 1c: Construct a scaffold-independent TAF blueprint specifying
required, preferred, and forbidden molecular features with 3D geometric
constraints and stereochemical requirements.

Sub-aim 1d: Validate TAF stability through leave-one-out cross-
validation, leave-one-active-out analysis, and multi-model comparison.

MILESTONE: TAF blueprint with confidence scores and transferability
hypotheses for each candidate feature. Target completion: Month 12.

SPECIFIC AIM 2: Identify novel α9α10 PAM scaffolds through
computationally guided discovery.

Sub-aim 2a: Screen purchasable chemical databases (ZINC22, Enamine
REAL) using TAF-compatible multi-objective scoring integrating 2D
feature compatibility, 3D shape matching, stereochemical analysis,
novelty, and diversity.

Sub-aim 2b: Generate novel scaffolds using REINVENT 4 with TAF-
constrained scoring functions in three modes: (i) R-group optimization
(control), (ii) scaffold hopping (primary discovery mode), and
(iii) de novo generation with TAF constraints.

Sub-aim 2c: Construct a diversified candidate portfolio with six
groups: (A) high-confidence transfer, (B) high novelty, (C) stereo-
chemical test pairs, (D) mechanistic test compounds, (E) exploratory
candidates, and (F) negative controls.

MILESTONE: Candidate portfolio of 50-100 prioritized compounds with
multi-objective scores. Target completion: Month 24.

SPECIFIC AIM 3: Experimentally validate TAF transferability.

Sub-aim 3a: Synthesize 15-20 selected candidates prioritized for
information gain (not merely predicted potency).

Sub-aim 3b: Test compounds using two-electrode voltage-clamp
electrophysiology at α9α10 nAChR-expressing Xenopus oocytes.

Sub-aim 3c: Update TAF confidence and transferability metrics with
new experimental data using active learning.

Sub-aim 3d: Iterate through discovery cycle to refine TAF blueprint
for subsequent experimental rounds.

MILESTONE: Experimental validation of at least 2 novel active scaffolds.
Target completion: Month 48.

MILESTONE: Updated TAF blueprint with quantitative transferability
scores. Target completion: Month 60.

APPROACH:

We will employ a multi-method computational approach integrating:
(1) molecular fingerprint analysis (Morgan, MACCS, RDKit); (2)
matched molecular pair analysis; (3) Random Forest, Gradient Boosting,
and Ridge classification with leave-one-out validation; (4)
counterfactual perturbation analysis; (5) feature ablation; (6) 3D
conformer generation and shape analysis; (7) REINVENT 4 generative
design; and (8) two-electrode voltage-clamp electrophysiology.

INNOVATION:

This project is innovative in three respects:
(1) The TAF framework is specifically designed for small-dataset drug
    discovery, unlike conventional QSAR which requires large datasets.
(2) TAFs are defined as 3D/stereochemical features, not merely 2D
    functional groups, enabling genuine scaffold hopping.
(3) The integrated computational-experimental active learning loop
    continuously improves feature transferability with each round of
    experimental testing.

IMPACT:

Success will demonstrate that small, well-designed datasets suffice
for identifying transferable molecular features, challenging the
prevailing assumption that large datasets are required for AI-driven
drug discovery. The methodology is generalizable to any underexplored
pharmacological target.

SECTION 3: RESEARCH STRATEGY

3.1 SIGNIFICANCE
========================================================================

3.1.1 Health Relevance

Chronic pain affects approximately 50 million adults in the United
States and represents a major public health burden with estimated
annual costs exceeding $560 billion (Dahlhamer et al., MMWR, 2018).
Current treatments—including opioids, NSAIDs, and anticonvulsants—
are limited by efficacy, tolerance, addiction potential, and adverse
effects. There is an urgent need for novel analgesic targets and
mechanisms.

The α9α10 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) has emerged as a
promising target for pain management. Unlike α7 nAChR which has been
extensively studied, α9α10 remains pharmacologically underexplored.
Recent studies have demonstrated that α9α10 nAChR is expressed in
dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons and modulates nociceptive signaling
(Bhatt et al., J Neurosci, 2020). Positive allosteric modulators
(PAMs) of α9α10 represent a mechanism to enhance cholinergic
analgesic signaling without direct receptor activation, potentially
offering improved selectivity and reduced side effects.

Beyond pain, α9α10 nAChR has been implicated in:
  - Inflammatory conditions (modulation of immune cell function)
  - Cochlear hearing disorders (expression in inner hair cells)
  - Neuropsychiatric disorders (expression in CNS circuits)

Despite this therapeutic potential, no FDA-approved α9α10 nAChR
modulators exist, and the chemical space remains largely unexplored.

3.1.2 The Small-Data Barrier

The central challenge in α9α10 nAChR drug discovery is the scarcity
of experimental data. Unlike well-studied targets (e.g., EGFR, ACE2)
with thousands of compounds, α9α10 has approximately 30 characterized
molecules. This creates a fundamental barrier:

  CONVENTIONAL QSAR: Requires hundreds to thousands of compounds for
  reliable model training. With 30 compounds, overfitting is
  inevitable and generalization claims are unjustified.

  TRADITIONAL MEDICINAL CHEMISTRY: Relies on experienced chemists'
  intuition, which may not transfer to novel targets without extensive
  historical data.

  DEEP LEARNING: Neural networks trained from scratch on 30 compounds
  will memorize rather than learn, producing misleading predictions.

This "small-data problem" is not unique to α9α10—it affects any
emerging pharmacological target, rare disease, or neglected tropical
disease where experimental data is inherently limited. A generalizable
solution would have broad impact across drug discovery.

3.1.3 Why Current Approaches Fail

The prevailing assumption in AI-driven drug discovery is that more
data yields better models. This assumption fails when:

  (a) The dataset is too small for the model complexity
  (b) The chemical space is too diverse for interpolation
  (c) Activity cliffs exist (small structural changes cause large
      activity changes)
  (d) The target is novel with no historical SAR knowledge

For α9α10 PAMs, all four conditions apply. The dataset is small (30
compounds), the series spans diverse scaffolds (17 unique Murcko
scaffolds), activity cliffs are observed (e.g., ID 12 vs ID 9:
>30,000-fold potency difference), and the target is novel.

3.1.4 The TAF Solution

We propose an alternative paradigm: Transferable Activity Features
(TAFs). Rather than building predictive models that require large
datasets, we identify molecular features that are:

  (a) Consistently associated with activity (stability)
  (b) Interpretable and testable (transparency)
  (c) Transferable across scaffolds (generalizability)
  (d) Defined in 3D with stereochemical context (specificity)

The TAF approach is specifically designed for the small-data regime
and does not require the statistical power needed for conventional
QSAR. Instead, it relies on convergence of multiple independent lines
of evidence—a principle borrowed from structural biology where
multiple low-resolution methods converge on a single structural
model.

3.1.5 Preliminary Data

Our pilot study of 30 α9α10 PAM compounds (7 active, 23 inactive)
demonstrates the feasibility of the TAF approach:

Finding 1: Five candidate TAFs were identified through convergence
of substructure enrichment, MMP analysis, counterfactual analysis,
and feature ablation.

Finding 2: Counterfactual analysis showed that removing the ethynyl
group from the most potent compound (ID 12, 0.1981 uM) resulted in
predicted inactivation with 88.5% confidence.

Finding 3: 3D shape analysis revealed that active compounds have
distinct elongated conformations (asphericity 0.414±0.123) compared
to inactive compounds (0.315±0.124).

Finding 4: Leave-one-out cross-validation achieved 86.7% accuracy
with Random Forest, providing internal consistency validation.

Finding 5: The series exhibits substantial scaffold diversity (17
unique Murcko scaffolds), suggesting that scaffold hopping is
feasible.

These preliminary findings establish the foundation for the proposed
research and demonstrate that the TAF framework can extract meaningful
features from extremely limited data.

3.1.6 Knowledge Gaps

Despite these promising preliminary results, critical knowledge gaps
remain:

  Gap 1: TAF transferability has not been tested across structurally
  distinct scaffolds. The hypothesis that these features can be
  transferred remains prospective.

  Gap 2: The 3D TAF definitions are based on ligand-only conformer
  analysis. Integration with receptor structural models (AlphaFold 3,
  Boltz-2) is needed to validate binding hypotheses.

  Gap 3: The relationship between TAF scores and experimental activity
  has not been established. A prospective validation experiment is
  required.

  Gap 4: The active learning loop—updating TAFs with new experimental
  data—has not been implemented.

This proposal directly addresses all four knowledge gaps through an
integrated computational-experimental program.

3.2 INNOVATION
========================================================================

3.2.1 Conceptual Innovation: The TAF Framework

The TAF framework represents a conceptual departure from conventional
drug discovery approaches:

  CONVENTIONAL QSAR:
  Input: Large dataset → Model → Predict → Select → Test
  Assumption: More data = better predictions
  Limitation: Fails with small datasets

  TAF APPROACH:
  Input: Small dataset → Feature identification → Transfer → Test → Update
  Assumption: Activity-associated features are transferable
  Advantage: Designed for small datasets

The TAF framework is not a replacement for QSAR but a complementary
approach specifically designed for the regime where QSAR fails.

3.2.2 Methodological Innovation: Multi-Method Convergence

Rather than relying on any single computational method, the TAF
framework requires convergence of multiple independent lines of
evidence:

  Evidence Source          What it provides
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
  Substructure enrichment  Chemical feature associations
  MMP analysis             Structural transformation effects
  Counterfactual analysis  Feature necessity evidence
  Feature ablation         Feature sufficiency evidence
  3D shape analysis        Spatial constraints
  Stereochemistry analysis Configuration requirements
  ML feature importance    Statistical support

A feature is considered a candidate TAF only when supported by at
least three independent evidence sources. This convergence principle
reduces the risk of false positives from any single method.

3.2.3 Definition Innovation: 3D/Stereochemical TAFs

Previous approaches to scaffold hopping typically use 2D fingerprints
or pharmacophore patterns. The TAF framework extends this by defining
features as:

  TAF = chemical feature + physicochemical identity + 3D location
        + spatial relationship + orientation + stereochemical context

For example, instead of "contains a hydroxyl group" (2D), the TAF
definition would be:

  "Hydroxyl group positioned approximately X Å from the lactone carbonyl
   oxygen with R configuration at the adjacent chiral center, oriented
   approximately Y° from the ring plane"

This 3D/stereochemical definition is essential for genuine scaffold
hopping because the same 2D functional group may adopt different 3D
orientations depending on the scaffold.

3.2.4 Validation Innovation: Counterfactual + Active Learning

The TAF framework incorporates two novel validation strategies:

  COUNTERFACTUAL VALIDATION: For each candidate TAF, we ask: "What
  minimal structural modification would destroy the activity-associated
  signal?" This provides direct evidence for feature necessity.

  ACTIVE LEARNING: After each experimental round, TAF confidence is
  updated and the next round of candidates is selected to maximize
  information gain (not merely predicted potency). This iterative
  refinement is more efficient than single-round screening.

3.2.5 Portfolio Innovation: Mechanistic Test Design

Unlike conventional drug discovery which selects candidates solely
by predicted potency, the TAF framework constructs a diversified
portfolio including:

  Group A: High-confidence transfer candidates
  Group B: High-novelty candidates
  Group C: Stereochemical test pairs (same structure, different stereo)
  Group D: Mechanistic test compounds (remove one TAF at a time)
  Group E: Exploratory candidates (high uncertainty)
  Group F: Negative controls (similar molecule lacking key TAF)

This portfolio design maximizes information gain per experimental
dollar, which is critical when resources are limited.

3.2.6 Comparison to Existing Methods

  METHOD              TAF FRAMEWORK ADVANTAGE
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Pharmacophore       TAFs include 3D + stereo + quantitative evidence
  QSAR                TAFs designed for n<50, not n>500
  GNN                 TAFs are interpretable, not black-box
  3D similarity       TAFs integrate multiple evidence sources
  Free energy pert.   TAFs do not require receptor structure
  Active learning     TAF framework includes mechanistic test design

3.3 APPROACH
========================================================================

3.3.1 Study Design Overview

This project employs an integrated computational-experimental design
organized into three interdependent aims executed over five years:

  YEAR 1          YEAR 2          YEAR 3          YEAR 4          YEAR 5
  ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐
  │ AIM 1    │   │ AIM 2    │   │ AIM 3    │   │ AIM 3    │   │ AIM 3    │
  │ TAF      │──▶│ Library  │──▶│ Synth &  │──▶│ Round 2  │──▶│ Round 3  │
  │ Discovery│   │ Screen & │   │ Test     │   │ Synth &  │   │ Synth &  │
  │          │   │ REINVENT │   │ Round 1  │   │ Test     │   │ Test     │
  └──────────┘   └──────────┘   └──────────┘   └──────────┘   └──────────┘
       │              │              │              │              │
       ▼              ▼              ▼              ▼              ▼
  TAF Blueprint   Candidate     Experimental   Updated TAF    Validated
  (M12)           Portfolio     Data           Blueprint      TAFs
                  (M24)         (M36)          (M48)          (M60)

The design follows a prospective hypothesis-testing paradigm:

  Step 1: Identify TAFs from initial dataset (AIM 1)
  Step 2: Use TAFs to select/generate test compounds (AIM 2)
  Step 3: Test compounds experimentally (AIM 3)
  Step 4: Update TAFs with new data (AIM 3)
  Step 5: Iterate until transferability is demonstrated

This iterative design is essential because TAF transferability is a
prospective hypothesis that can only be validated through experimental
testing on structurally distinct scaffolds.

3.3.2 Study Population and Sample Size

COMPOUNDS (not biological subjects):

  Training set: 30 compounds (7 active, 23 inactive)
  Screening set: ~1,000,000 compounds (ZINC22, Enamine REAL)
  Generated set: ~10,000 compounds (REINVENT 4)
  Candidate set: 50-100 prioritized compounds
  Test set: 15-20 compounds per experimental round
  Total experimental: 45-60 compounds over 3 rounds

SAMPLE SIZE JUSTIFICATION:

For the initial dataset (n=30, 7 active), we note that:
  - Fisher exact test has power >0.8 for enrichment >3-fold
  - Leave-one-out CV provides unbiased error estimation
  - The convergence principle (multiple methods) compensates for
    limited statistical power of individual methods

For experimental validation (n=15-20 per round), we estimate:
  - If 30% of candidates are active (based on TAF filtering), we
    expect 4-6 actives per round
  - Three rounds provide 12-18 active compounds for TAF refinement
  - This is sufficient to estimate T(F) with standard error <0.15

3.3.3 Methods

METHOD 1: COMPREHENSIVE CHEMICAL ANALYSIS (AIM 1)

Objective: Characterize the starting dataset and identify initial
activity-associated features.

Approach:

Step 1.1: Data Discovery and Quality Control
  - Parse all SMILES using RDKit (v2025.09.4)
  - Canonicalize structures while preserving stereochemistry
  - Calculate molecular descriptors: MW, LogP, TPSA, HBD, HBA,
    RotBonds, FractionCSP3, RingCount, AromaticRings
  - Identify duplicates (exact and connectivity)
  - Flag undefined stereochemistry
  - Produce data_qc.csv and quality control report

Step 1.2: Activity Representation
  - Classify compounds as active/inactive based on potency and
    potentiation thresholds
  - Calculate pPotency = -log10(Activity_uM) for non-zero values
  - Create normalized potentiation scores
  - Compute joint activity scores combining potency and potentiation
  - Rank compounds by potency

Step 1.3: Fingerprint Generation and Similarity Analysis
  - Generate Morgan fingerprints (radius 1, 2, 3; 2048 bits)
  - Generate MACCS keys (167 bits)
  - Generate RDKit topological fingerprints (2048 bits)
  - Compute pairwise Tanimoto similarity matrices
  - Perform PCA and t-SNE for chemical space visualization
  - Perform hierarchical clustering (Ward linkage)

Step 1.4: Scaffold Analysis
  - Murcko scaffold decomposition
  - Maximum common substructure (MCS) identification
  - Scaffold diversity analysis
  - R-group decomposition where applicable

Step 1.5: Matched Molecular Pair Analysis
  - Identify MMP pairs (same scaffold, Tanimoto ≥ 0.5)
  - Calculate activity differences (Δ potency, Δ potentiation)
  - Classify transformations as gain/loss/no-change

Step 1.6: Feature Enrichment Analysis
  - Define 10+ substructure patterns (SMARTS)
  - Calculate enrichment in active vs inactive compounds
  - Fisher exact test for statistical significance
  - Rank features by enrichment and evidence strength

Step 1.7: Machine Learning Validation
  - Random Forest classification (n=100, class_weight='balanced')
  - Gradient Boosting classification (n=50)
  - Ridge classification (α=1.0)
  - Leave-one-out cross-validation
  - Leave-one-active-out analysis
  - Feature importance analysis (Gini importance)

Step 1.8: Counterfactual Analysis
  - For most potent compound: remove ethynyl group
  - For most potent compound: remove stereochemistry
  - For second most potent: remove bromine
  - Re-classify modified structures using trained model
  - Report prediction and confidence

Step 1.9: Feature Ablation
  - Systematically zero out each molecular descriptor
  - Measure accuracy decrease relative to baseline
  - Identify descriptors that are individually necessary

Step 1.10: 3D Conformer Analysis
  - Generate 3D conformers using ETKDGv3
  - Optimize with MMFF force field
  - Calculate shape descriptors: Asphericity, Eccentricity,
    NPR1, NPR2, SpherocityIndex
  - Compare active vs inactive shape distributions

Step 1.11: TAF Blueprint Construction
  - Integrate evidence from all methods
  - Define required, preferred, and forbidden features
  - Specify 3D geometric constraints
  - Specify stereochemical requirements
  - Assign confidence scores to each TAF

DELIVERABLES:
  - data_qc.csv, data_cleaned.csv
  - Activity representation table
  - Similarity matrices (3 files)
  - MMP pairs table
  - TAF evidence table
  - TAF blueprint table
  - Feature ablation results
  - Counterfactual results
  - 5 publication-quality figures
  - Comprehensive analysis report

METHOD 2: LIBRARY SCREENING AND GENERATIVE DESIGN (AIM 2)

Objective: Identify novel scaffolds using TAF-guided screening and
generative design.

Sub-aim 2a: Purchasable Library Screening

  Step 2.1: Database Acquisition
    - Download ZINC22 subset (drug-like, ≤500 Da)
    - Download Enamine REAL subset
    - Apply PAINS/Reactive group filters
    - Apply Lipinski Rule of Five filter
    - Apply molecular weight filter (150-500 Da)

  Step 2.2: Pre-screening
    - Calculate Morgan fingerprints for all compounds
    - Calculate molecular descriptors
    - Apply TAF-compatible property filters (MW 175-300, etc.)
    - Reduce candidate set to ~100,000 compounds

  Step 2.3: TAF Scoring
    - Calculate 2D TAF compatibility (substructure matching)
    - Calculate 3D shape compatibility (asphericity, eccentricity)
    - Calculate stereochemical compatibility
    - Calculate novelty (Tanimoto to training set)
    - Multi-objective scoring: S = w₁·TAF + w₂·3D + w₃·Stereo +
      w₄·Novelty + w₅·Diversity - w₆·Uncertainty

  Step 2.4: Diversity Selection
    - Pareto optimization on multi-objective scores
    - Maximize scaffold diversity among selected candidates
    - Select top 30-50 compounds

Sub-aim 2b: REINVENT 4 Generative Design

  Step 2.5: Configuration
    - MODE A: R-group optimization (control)
      * Maintain original scaffold
      * Optimize potency-associated features
    - MODE B: Scaffold hopping (primary mode)
      * Replace original core
      * Retain TAF blueprint
      * Preserve stereochemical hypotheses
    - MODE C: De novo generation
      * No scaffold constraints
      * Optimize TAF compatibility + novelty

  Step 2.6: TAF-Constrained Scoring Function
    - TAF score: presence of required features
    - 3D score: shape matching to active compounds
    - Stereo score: defined stereochemistry
    - Novelty score: distance from training set
    - Drug-likeness score: Lipinski, PAINS filters
    - Diversity score: distance from already generated molecules

  Step 2.7: Generation and Selection
    - Generate ~10,000 molecules per mode
    - Apply filters (validity, drug-likeness, TAF threshold)
    - Multi-objective scoring
    - Select top 30-50 compounds

Sub-aim 2c: Candidate Portfolio Construction

  Step 2.8: Portfolio Assembly
    - Group A: High-confidence TAF transfer (10-15 compounds)
    - Group B: High novelty with TAF compatibility (10-15)
    - Group C: Stereochemical test pairs (5 pairs)
    - Group D: Mechanistic test compounds (5-10)
      * Remove hydroxyl
      * Remove EWG
      * Invert stereochemistry
      * Modify lactone ring

    - Group F: Negative controls (5-10)

  Step 2.9: Common Evaluator
    - All candidates (purchasable + REINVENT) enter same pipeline

METHOD 3: EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION (AIM 3)

Objective: Validate TAF transferability through prospective testing.

Sub-aim 3a: Compound Selection and Synthesis

  Step 3.1: Prioritization
    - Rank candidates by information gain score
    - Include representatives from all portfolio groups
    - Prioritize chemically distinct scaffolds
    - Include negative controls
    - Select 15-20 compounds per round

  Step 3.2: Synthesis
    - Synthesize selected compounds (collaborative effort)
    - Confirm identity by NMR and mass spectrometry
    - Confirm purity by HPLC (>95%)

Sub-aim 3b: Electrophysiology Testing

  Step 3.3: Expression System
    - Clone human α9 and α10 nAChR subunits
    - Express in Xenopus laevis oocytes
    - Two-electrode voltage-clamp recording

  Step 3.4: Functional Assay
    - Measure ACh-evoked currents
    - Test PAM activity (co-application with EC₂₀ ACh)
    - Determine EC₅₀ for potentiation
    - Determine maximal potentiation (%)
    - Compare to reference compound (ID 12)

  Step 3.5: Selectivity Assessment
    - Test against α7 nAChR (counter-screen)
    - Test against α4β2 nAChR (counter-screen)
    - Assess selectivity ratio

Sub-aim 3c: Active Learning Update

  Step 3.6: Data Integration
    - Append new experimental data to training set
    - Recalculate TAF enrichment scores
    - Update confidence intervals
    - Recalculate transferability metrics:
      T(F) = active diverse scaffolds containing F /
             tested diverse scaffolds containing F

  Step 3.7: TAF Refinement
    - Identify which TAFs were validated by new data
    - Identify which TAFs need modification
    - Update 3D geometric constraints
    - Update stereochemical requirements

Sub-aim 3d: Iterative Discovery

  Step 3.8: Round 2 Design (Months 36-48)

    - Prioritize scaffolds not yet tested
    - Include mechanistic compounds to test specific TAFs
    - Synthesize and test 15-20 compounds

  Step 3.9: Round 3 Design (Months 48-60)
    - Final refinement of TAFs
    - Select final candidates for publication
    - Include best validated leads

3.3.4 Potential Pitfalls and Limitations

PITFALL 1: Low hit rate in library screening
  Risk: TAF blueprint may be too restrictive or too permissive
  Mitigation: Adjust TAF thresholds iteratively; include diverse
  candidate groups; use multiple TAF scoring approaches

PITFALL 2: Synthetic inaccessibility of selected candidates
  Risk: Top-scoring compounds may be difficult to synthesize
  Mitigation: Include synthetic feasibility in scoring; maintain
  close collaboration with synthetic chemists; prioritize
  commercially available or easily synthesized scaffolds

PITFALL 3: Compounds inactive at α9α10 despite TAF compatibility
  Risk: TAFs may not transfer across scaffolds
  Mitigation: This is the central hypothesis to be tested; include
  negative controls to quantify false positive rate; update TAFs
  with active learning

PITFALL 4: Insufficient statistical power for feature validation
  Risk: Small dataset may not support robust statistical tests
  Mitigation: Use convergence of multiple methods; Bayesian
  updating with new experimental data; focus on effect sizes
  rather than p-values

PITFALL 5: REINVENT 4 generates invalid or unrealistic molecules
  Risk: Generative model may produce chemically unreasonable
  structures
  Mitigation: Apply rigorous validity filters; include synthetic
  feasibility in scoring; manual review by medicinal chemist

3.3.5 Alternative Approaches

ALTERNATIVE 1: Use only conventional QSAR (rejected)
  Reason: With n=30, overfitting is inevitable and generalization
  claims are scientifically unjustified. The TAF approach is
  specifically designed for this regime.

ALTERNATIVE 2: Use only 3D pharmacophore modeling (rejected)
  Reason: Requires receptor structure which is not available at
  sufficient resolution. Ligand-based 3D approaches are more
  appropriate.

ALTERNATIVE 3: Use only purchased compounds without generative
design (rejected)
  Reason: Purchasable libraries may not contain sufficient scaffold
  diversity for genuine scaffold hopping. REINVENT 4 enables
  exploration of novel chemical space.

ALTERNATIVE 4: Skip experimental validation and rely on computational
predictions (rejected)
  Reason: The central hypothesis (TAF transferability) can only be
  validated experimentally. Computational predictions without
  experimental validation do not constitute scientific evidence.

3.4 PROTECTIVE FACTOR FOR RIGOR AND REPRODUCIBILITY
========================================================================

To ensure rigor and reproducibility, this project incorporates the
following protective factors:

1. MULTIPLE INDEPENDENT METHODS: TAF identification requires
   convergence of ≥3 independent evidence sources, reducing false
   positives from any single method.

2. LEAVE-ONE-OUT VALIDATION: All models are validated using LOO-CV,
   which provides unbiased error estimation for small datasets.

3. LEAVE-ONE-ACTIVE-OUT ANALYSIS: Specifically tests whether
   conclusions depend on individual active compounds.

4. COUNTERFACTUAL VALIDATION: Tests feature necessity by asking
   what modifications would destroy activity.

5. FEATURE ABLATION: Tests feature sufficiency by removing individual
   features and measuring performance impact.

6. NEGATIVE CONTROLS: Each candidate portfolio includes compounds
   designed to test specific TAFs.

7. PROSPECTIVE HYPOTHESIS TESTING: TAF transferability is stated
   as a prospective hypothesis that requires experimental validation.

8. ACTIVE LEARNING LOOP: Experimental results update TAF confidence,
   preventing over-interpretation of initial computational findings.

9. RANDOM SEED CONTROL: All computational analyses use fixed random
   seeds for reproducibility.

10. CODE AND DATA SHARING: All analysis scripts, data, and figures
    will be publicly available in a GitHub repository.

11. BLINDED ANALYSIS: Where feasible, compound identities will be
    blinded during initial computational analysis to prevent
    confirmation bias.

12. MULTIPLE COMPOUNDS PER HYPOTHESIS: Each TAF is tested by
    multiple compounds, not just one, reducing the risk of
    false conclusions from individual molecules.
